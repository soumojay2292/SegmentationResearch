Skin cancer is among the most frequently diagnosed malignancies worldwide, and melanoma, although less common than the keratinocyte carcinomas, accounts for a disproportionate share of the associated mortality. The prognosis of melanoma depends strongly upon the stage at which the disease is identified, and lesions detected while still confined to the epidermis are associated with markedly more favourable outcomes than those detected after invasion has occurred. Early and accurate identification of suspicious pigmented lesions is therefore of considerable clinical importance, and the diagnostic burden falls upon a specialist workforce that is limited relative to the volume of lesions requiring assessment. Computational methods that support the examination of pigmented lesions have consequently attracted sustained attention.

Dermoscopy is a non-invasive imaging technique that suppresses surface reflection and renders subsurface pigmented structures visible, and it has become a routine modality in the clinical assessment of skin lesions. Within the analysis of dermoscopic images, segmentation of the lesion region is a foundational step. The delineated region determines the measured extent and shape of the lesion, and these in turn underpin the morphological criteria used in clinical assessment, including the asymmetry, border irregularity, and diameter that feature in established diagnostic rules. Segmentation likewise conditions the performance of downstream classification systems, which frequently operate on the lesion region or on features derived from it. Manual delineation, however, is laborious and exhibits appreciable inter-observer variability, particularly along the lesion margin, which motivates the development of automated methods.

The delineation of the lesion boundary is the principal difficulty of this task. Dermoscopic images frequently exhibit low contrast between the lesion and the surrounding skin, so that the transition between the two is gradual rather than abrupt and the margin cannot be located from local intensity gradients alone. Lesion margins are moreover often diffuse or irregular in outline, and the pigmented region may fade progressively into adjacent skin over an extended transition zone. The images are further affected by structures that are not part of the lesion, including hair, air bubbles, ruler markings, and vascular patterns, which may traverse or obscure the margin. Lesions themselves vary widely in scale, shape, colour, and texture, and a single image may contain more than one pigmented structure, of which only one is the object of interest. These conditions bear most heavily upon the boundary region, where the evidence distinguishing lesion from background is weakest.

Automated approaches to this problem have developed along two principal lines. Convolutional encoder–decoder architectures, of which the U-Net is the archetype, established the pattern of contracting and expanding paths joined by skip connections, and subsequent variants refined this design through attention gating of the skip connections, through nested and densely connected decoding paths, and through a variety of multi-scale aggregation schemes. These methods capture local structure effectively, but the limited receptive field of convolution constrains their access to long-range context. Architectures incorporating self-attention were introduced in response, either replacing the convolutional encoder with a vision transformer or combining convolutional and transformer components in hybrid encoders, thereby admitting global context at the cost of substantially greater computational and data requirements. More recently, large-scale visual foundation models trained on very large and diverse image collections have been adapted to medical segmentation, most prominently the Segment Anything Model family, whose image encoders provide transferable representations that reduce the dependence on scarce annotated medical data.

Each of these lines of work leaves difficulties that bear directly upon boundary delineation. Foundation models are trained predominantly on natural images and are separated from dermoscopy by a domain gap, so that their representations require adaptation before they transfer effectively; the adaptation mechanism, rather than the backbone, then determines how well fine structure is preserved. More generally, the great majority of segmentation methods are both optimised and evaluated with region-overlap criteria. Such criteria are area-based, and the neighbourhood of the contour occupies only a small fraction of the area of a compact lesion; a contour displaced by a small distance alters the overlapping area only slightly. Optimisation and evaluation conducted on this basis therefore exert limited pressure upon precisely the region in which dermoscopic segmentation is most difficult, and may fail to distinguish methods that differ appreciably in the fidelity of the contours they produce. Where boundary-sensitive information is treated at all, it is commonly handled implicitly, being combined with contextual information within a single undifferentiated representation in which the low-frequency content describing the lesion interior predominates.

These considerations motivate an approach in which boundary-relevant information is represented explicitly and assessed explicitly. The frequency domain affords a natural means of the former, since the intensity transitions associated with lesion margins are represented in the higher-frequency portion of the spectrum and may be separated there from the lower-frequency content describing the lesion interior more directly than in a single spatial representation. Assessment requires measures defined on the contour itself, since region-overlap criteria alone cannot characterise the property of interest. The Original MAFF-Net addressed the adaptation of a frozen foundation backbone to dermoscopy through multi-scale perception adaptation and frequency-guided feature fusion, together with an auxiliary reconstruction branch, and demonstrated that spatial and spectral information can be integrated effectively within such a framework. It does not, however, maintain the spatial and frequency representations as separate streams that are reconciled before being combined, which invites investigation of an explicit dual-stream formulation.

The present work accordingly pursues a targeted refinement of the MAFF-Net framework rather than its replacement. The pretrained encoder, the convolutional decoder, and the auxiliary reconstruction branch are retained from the Original MAFF-Net, and the redesign is confined to the pathway in which spatial and spectral information are combined. In the proposed formulation the multi-scale features produced by the frozen encoder are routed simultaneously along a spatial stream, which preserves regional and contextual structure, and a frequency stream, which emphasises boundary-sensitive spectral content. The two streams are brought onto a common statistical footing before being combined, and are then merged by a learned gate whose weighting is determined from the features themselves rather than fixed in advance. Training is conducted under a region objective supplemented by auxiliary boundary-focused supervision applied to the segmentation outputs and to the reconstruction branch.

The contributions of this work are as follows. First, a dual-stream fusion pathway is introduced for the adaptation of a frozen foundation backbone to dermoscopic segmentation, comprising a parameter-free Frequency Branch that provides an explicit spectral route for boundary-sensitive content, a Feature Alignment Module that reconciles the statistics of the spatial and spectral representations prior to their combination, and a Gated Fusion Module that combines the two aligned representations by a learned, spatially varying weighting. Second, a boundary-centric supervision strategy is formulated as an auxiliary complement to the primary region objective, directing part of the training signal towards the lesion contour. Third, the three architectural components are examined individually through a leave-one-out ablation in which each is disabled in turn while the remainder of the network and the training configuration are held fixed. Fourth, the evaluation is conducted with region-based and boundary-based measures in combination across three public dermoscopic benchmarks, an arrangement that permits the behaviour of the contour to be assessed separately from the extent of the predicted region. The encoder, decoder, and reconstruction branch are adopted from the Original MAFF-Net and are not claimed as contributions of this work.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section II reviews related work on dermoscopic lesion segmentation. Section III describes the proposed methodology. Section IV specifies the datasets, implementation, evaluation measures, and experimental protocol. Section V reports the experimental results, Section VI discusses their interpretation and the limitations of the study, and Section VII concludes.

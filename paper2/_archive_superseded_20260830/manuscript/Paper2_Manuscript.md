# A Dual-Stream Spatial–Spectral Fusion Pathway for Boundary Fidelity in Dermoscopic Skin Lesion Segmentation

## Abstract

Accurate delineation of lesion boundaries remains a fundamental difficulty in dermoscopic image segmentation, where gradual lesion-to-skin transitions, irregular margins, and occluding structures such as hair make the contour the least certain part of any prediction. Segmentation methods are nevertheless optimised and assessed predominantly by region-overlap criteria, which are area-based and respond only weakly to displacement of the contour; boundary-relevant content is commonly merged with contextual information in a single representation dominated by the lesion interior. This paper presents a targeted refinement of the MAFF-Net framework in which the pretrained encoder, decoder, and auxiliary reconstruction branch are retained and only the fusion pathway is redesigned. Multi-scale features from a frozen foundation backbone are processed along two parallel streams, a spatial stream that preserves contextual structure and a parameter-free frequency stream that emphasises boundary-sensitive spectral content; their statistics are reconciled before they are merged by a learned, spatially varying gate. The method was evaluated on three public dermoscopic benchmarks against four segmentation architectures under an identical protocol, using region-based and boundary-based measures in combination, and each introduced component was isolated by a leave-one-out ablation. Removing any component degraded all boundary measures while leaving region overlap substantially unchanged. The contribution is accordingly characterised as an improvement in boundary fidelity rather than region overlap, and indicates that region-overlap criteria alone are insufficient to assess methods acting upon the lesion margin. All results derive from single training runs, and no claim of statistical significance is made.

## Keywords

Skin lesion segmentation; dermoscopy; boundary fidelity; frequency-domain representation; gated feature fusion; frozen foundation model; deep supervision; ISIC.

# I. Introduction

Skin cancer is among the most frequently diagnosed malignancies worldwide, and melanoma, although less common than the keratinocyte carcinomas, accounts for a disproportionate share of the associated mortality [1]. The prognosis of melanoma depends strongly upon the stage at which the disease is identified, and lesions detected while still confined to the epidermis are associated with markedly more favourable outcomes than those detected after invasion has occurred. Early and accurate identification of suspicious pigmented lesions is therefore of considerable clinical importance, and the diagnostic burden falls upon a specialist workforce that is limited relative to the volume of lesions requiring assessment. Computational methods that support the examination of pigmented lesions have consequently attracted sustained attention.

Dermoscopy is a non-invasive imaging technique that suppresses surface reflection and renders subsurface pigmented structures visible, and it has become a routine modality in the clinical assessment of skin lesions [2]. Within the analysis of dermoscopic images, segmentation of the lesion region is a foundational step. The delineated region determines the measured extent and shape of the lesion, and these in turn underpin the morphological criteria used in clinical assessment, including the asymmetry, border irregularity, and diameter that feature in established diagnostic rules [3]. Segmentation likewise conditions the performance of downstream classification systems, which frequently operate on the lesion region or on features derived from it. Manual delineation, however, is laborious and exhibits appreciable inter-observer variability, particularly along the lesion margin, which motivates the development of automated methods.

The delineation of the lesion boundary is the principal difficulty of this task. Dermoscopic images frequently exhibit low contrast between the lesion and the surrounding skin, so that the transition between the two is gradual rather than abrupt and the margin cannot be located from local intensity gradients alone. Lesion margins are moreover often diffuse or irregular in outline, and the pigmented region may fade progressively into adjacent skin over an extended transition zone. The images are further affected by structures that are not part of the lesion, including hair, air bubbles, ruler markings, and vascular patterns, which may traverse or obscure the margin [2]. Lesions themselves vary widely in scale, shape, colour, and texture, and a single image may contain more than one pigmented structure, of which only one is the object of interest. These conditions bear most heavily upon the boundary region, where the evidence distinguishing lesion from background is weakest.

Automated approaches to this problem have developed along two principal lines. Convolutional encoder–decoder architectures, of which the U-Net is the archetype [4], established the pattern of contracting and expanding paths joined by skip connections, and subsequent variants refined this design through attention gating of the skip connections [5], through nested and densely connected decoding paths [6], and through a variety of multi-scale aggregation schemes. These methods capture local structure effectively, but the limited receptive field of convolution constrains their access to long-range context. Architectures incorporating self-attention were introduced in response, either replacing the convolutional encoder with a vision transformer [7] or combining convolutional and transformer components in hybrid encoders [8], thereby admitting global context at the cost of substantially greater computational and data requirements. More recently, large-scale visual foundation models trained on very large and diverse image collections have been adapted to medical segmentation, most prominently the Segment Anything Model family [9], [10], whose image encoders provide transferable representations that reduce the dependence on scarce annotated medical data.

Each of these lines of work leaves difficulties that bear directly upon boundary delineation. Foundation models are trained predominantly on natural images and are separated from dermoscopy by a domain gap [11], so that their representations require adaptation before they transfer effectively; the adaptation mechanism, rather than the backbone, then determines how well fine structure is preserved. More generally, the great majority of segmentation methods are both optimised and evaluated with region-overlap criteria. Such criteria are area-based, and the neighbourhood of the contour occupies only a small fraction of the area of a compact lesion; a contour displaced by a small distance alters the overlapping area only slightly [12]. Optimisation and evaluation conducted on this basis therefore exert limited pressure upon precisely the region in which dermoscopic segmentation is most difficult, and may fail to distinguish methods that differ appreciably in the fidelity of the contours they produce. Where boundary-sensitive information is treated at all, it is commonly handled implicitly, being combined with contextual information within a single undifferentiated representation in which the low-frequency content describing the lesion interior predominates.

These considerations motivate an approach in which boundary-relevant information is represented explicitly and assessed explicitly. The frequency domain affords a natural means of the former, since the intensity transitions associated with lesion margins are represented in the higher-frequency portion of the spectrum [13], [14] and may be separated there from the lower-frequency content describing the lesion interior more directly than in a single spatial representation. Assessment requires measures defined on the contour itself, since region-overlap criteria alone cannot characterise the property of interest. The Original MAFF-Net [15] addressed the adaptation of a frozen foundation backbone to dermoscopy through multi-scale perception adaptation and frequency-guided feature fusion, together with an auxiliary reconstruction branch, and demonstrated that spatial and spectral information can be integrated effectively within such a framework. It does not, however, maintain the spatial and frequency representations as separate streams that are reconciled before being combined, which invites investigation of an explicit dual-stream formulation.

The present work accordingly investigates a targeted dual-stream refinement of the fusion stage of the MAFF-Net framework, rather than a replacement of the framework as a whole. The pretrained encoder, the convolutional decoder, and the auxiliary reconstruction branch are retained from the Original MAFF-Net, and the redesign is confined to the pathway in which spatial and spectral information are combined. In this formulation the multi-scale features produced by the frozen encoder are routed simultaneously along a spatial stream, which preserves regional and contextual structure, and a frequency stream, which emphasises boundary-sensitive spectral content; the two streams are brought onto a common statistical footing before being combined, and are then merged by a learned gate whose weighting is determined from the features themselves rather than fixed in advance. Training is conducted under a region objective supplemented by auxiliary boundary-focused supervision applied to the segmentation outputs and to the reconstruction branch. The resulting model is then examined, alongside standard convolutional and transformer baselines, under a protocol that reports region-based and boundary-based measures in combination, so that the effect of the design on the lesion contour can be assessed separately from its effect on the extent of the predicted region.

The contributions of this work are as follows. First, it is shown that region-overlap metrics alone are insufficient to characterise methods that act on the lesion margin: evaluating the models on the held-out test splits of three public dermoscopic benchmarks with region-based and boundary-based measures in combination reveals that the two families of measures induce different model rankings, so that boundary quality must be assessed with contour-based measures rather than inferred from region overlap. Second, the proposed dual-stream model — which combines an explicit parameter-free Frequency Branch, per-stream Feature Alignment, and a learned Gated Fusion on a frozen foundation backbone — is shown to attain higher region-based and boundary-based scores than four standard convolutional and transformer baselines on all three benchmarks under an identical single-run protocol. Third, the dual-stream reformulation of the fusion pathway is investigated, and the marginal role of each of its three components is analysed through a leave-one-out ablation in which each component is disabled in turn while the remainder of the network and the training configuration are held fixed; removing any component is found to degrade all boundary measures while leaving region overlap essentially unchanged on the dataset examined, indicating that the effect of these components is confined to boundary fidelity rather than to the extent of the predicted region. The encoder, decoder, and reconstruction branch are adopted from the Original MAFF-Net and are not claimed as contributions of this work; the boundary-centric supervision under which the network is trained is described in Section III-H as part of the training objective.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section II reviews related work on dermoscopic lesion segmentation. Section III describes the proposed methodology. Section IV specifies the datasets, implementation, evaluation measures, and experimental protocol. Section V reports the experimental results, Section VI discusses their interpretation and the limitations of the study, and Section VII concludes.

# II. Related Work

II-A. Convolutional Segmentation Architectures

The formulation of segmentation as dense per-pixel classification by fully convolutional networks [16] established the basis upon which subsequent architectures were constructed. The U-Net [4] adapted this formulation to biomedical imaging by pairing a contracting encoder with a symmetric expanding decoder and joining the two by skip connections, so that the fine spatial detail lost to successive downsampling could be reintroduced during reconstruction. This design proved well suited to the small annotated datasets characteristic of medical imaging and remains the reference point for the field. Later work observed that the direct concatenation of encoder and decoder features couples representations of differing semantic level, and UNet++ [6] accordingly interposed a nested arrangement of dense convolutional blocks along the skip pathways in order to reduce this disparity. These architectures recover the lesion region reliably, but the locality of the convolution operator restricts the spatial extent over which evidence can be aggregated, which is a limitation where the margin must be inferred from context beyond its immediate neighbourhood.

II-B. Attention Mechanisms

Attention mechanisms were introduced into this setting as a means of weighting features according to their informativeness rather than treating them uniformly. Squeeze-and-excitation blocks [17] recalibrate feature channels by a globally pooled descriptor, allowing informative channels to be emphasised. The Attention U-Net [5] applies a related principle spatially, gating each skip connection so that regions irrelevant to the target are suppressed before the encoder features are passed to the decoder. Such mechanisms improve the selectivity of the representation, and the gating principle they establish — that the contribution of a feature should be determined from the feature itself rather than fixed in advance — is directly relevant to the combination of heterogeneous representations. They do not, however, alter the receptive field of the underlying convolutions, and the constraint on long-range aggregation persists.

II-C. Transformer-Based Segmentation

The vision transformer [7] addressed this constraint by dispensing with convolution in favour of self-attention over image patches, admitting global interactions at every layer. Because pure transformer encoders forgo the inductive biases that make convolutional networks efficient on limited data, hybrid designs were proposed for medical segmentation. TransUNet [8] employs a convolutional encoder followed by transformer layers and retains a convolutional decoder, thereby combining local feature extraction with global context modelling, while Swin-UNet [18] constructs an encoder–decoder entirely from hierarchical windowed attention blocks. These architectures capture context effectively, but they carry substantially greater computational and data requirements, which is a material consideration given the size of publicly available dermoscopic collections.

II-D. Foundation Models for Medical Segmentation

The difficulty of training large models on limited annotated data has motivated the adaptation of visual foundation models pretrained on very large and diverse collections. The Segment Anything Model [9] provides a promptable architecture whose image encoder yields broadly transferable representations, and its successor [10] extends the family with a hierarchical encoder and improved efficiency. Direct application to medical images is nevertheless impaired by the domain gap between natural and medical imagery, and adaptation is required; MedSAM [11] addresses this by fine-tuning on a large assembled corpus of medical images. Fine-tuning an encoder of this scale is costly and, on small datasets, carries a risk of overfitting, which has led to approaches that retain the encoder in a frozen state and confine the trainable capacity to modules operating on its outputs. Under such a scheme the adaptation mechanism, rather than the backbone, governs how faithfully fine structure is preserved.

II-E. Frequency-Domain Learning

A complementary line of work processes features in the frequency domain rather than the spatial domain alone. Fast Fourier convolution [13] incorporates a spectral branch within the convolutional block so that a global receptive field is obtained at modest cost, and global filter networks [14] replace self-attention outright with learnable filters applied to the Fourier transform of the feature map. The interest of these methods for boundary delineation lies in the correspondence between spectral content and spatial structure: abrupt intensity transitions of the kind associated with object margins are represented predominantly in the higher-frequency components, whereas the smoothly varying appearance of homogeneous regions is concentrated in the lower-frequency components. Operating in this domain therefore affords a means of addressing margin-related content more directly than is possible within a single spatial representation.

II-F. Boundary-Aware Segmentation

Recognition that region-overlap objectives exert limited pressure upon the contour has prompted the development of boundary-aware formulations. Boundary loss [19] supplements a region objective with a distance-weighted term computed from the ground-truth contour, and related work has sought to reduce contour distances directly during optimisation. A parallel concern arises in evaluation: region-overlap criteria are area-based and are correspondingly insensitive to modest displacements of the contour, so that methods differing appreciably in margin fidelity may be assigned similar scores. The Boundary IoU measure [12] was proposed in response, restricting the overlap computation to a band about the contour so that boundary quality is assessed separately from region extent. Distance-based measures such as the Hausdorff distance and the average symmetric surface distance serve a comparable purpose in medical image analysis.

II-G. Summary and Research Gap

These lines of work converge upon the framework from which the present study proceeds. The Original MAFF-Net [15] adapts a frozen foundation backbone to dermoscopic segmentation through multi-scale perception adaptation and frequency-guided feature fusion, together with an auxiliary reconstruction branch, and demonstrates that spatial and spectral information can be integrated productively within a single framework. Three observations nevertheless remain. First, in that design and in comparable approaches the spectral and spatial content are combined within one fusion route rather than being maintained as separate streams; representations of markedly different statistical character are thereby merged without prior reconciliation, and the low-frequency content describing the lesion interior may predominate. Second, where two heterogeneous representations are combined, the weighting between them is commonly fixed rather than determined from the features themselves, notwithstanding that the gating principle established in the attention literature is directly applicable. Third, and cutting across the foregoing, methods intended to improve margin delineation are still assessed principally by region-overlap criteria, which, for the reasons set out above, are ill-suited to detecting the very property such methods are designed to improve.

The present work addresses these three points together. It retains the frozen foundation backbone and the established decoding pathway, separates the spatial and spectral representations into parallel streams, reconciles their statistics before combination, and merges them by a learned gate whose weighting varies with the features presented to it. It further evaluates the resulting model with region-based and boundary-based measures in combination, so that the effect upon the contour may be observed independently of the effect upon region extent.

# III. Methodology

III-A. Overall Framework of the Proposed MAFF-Net

Skin cancer is among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, and melanoma in particular carries a high mortality risk when detected late. Dermoscopy is a routine modality for examining pigmented skin lesions, and accurate delineation of the lesion region is a prerequisite for downstream tasks such as morphological assessment, malignancy scoring, and treatment planning. Manual delineation by dermatologists is time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability, which motivates reliable automated segmentation. This task remains difficult because dermoscopic images frequently exhibit low contrast between lesion and surrounding skin, gradual or ill-defined boundaries, occlusion by hair and vascular structures, and pronounced variability in lesion shape, scale, and pigmentation. Segmentation methods intended for clinical support must therefore remain robust under these conditions and, in particular, must recover lesion boundaries faithfully.

Large-scale visual foundation models provide transferable representations that reduce the dependence on scarce annotated medical data, but their direct application to dermoscopy is limited by the domain gap between natural and medical images. The Original MAFF-Net [15] addresses this gap by adapting a pretrained visual foundation backbone with multi-scale perception adaptation (MMPA) and frequency-guided feature fusion (FGFF), and by regularizing the decoder through an auxiliary Reconstruction Decoder Branch (RDB). This design integrates spatial and spectral information effectively; it does not, however, explicitly separate the spatial and frequency representations prior to fusion, which motivates further investigation into explicit dual-stream integration in which the two domains are treated as separate, reconciled streams.

Accordingly, the present work pursues a targeted refinement of the MAFF-Net framework rather than its replacement, retaining the validated components of the Original MAFF-Net and redesigning only the fusion pathway where explicit dual-stream integration is justified. In keeping with this principle, the frozen encoder, the convolutional decoder, and the auxiliary reconstruction branch are preserved without alteration, so that the strong transferable priors and the proven decoding pathway continue to anchor the network. In place of the original single fusion pathway, the proposed framework introduces a dual-stream formulation in which the multi-scale features produced by the encoder are processed along two parallel routes: a spatial stream that preserves regional and contextual structure, and a frequency stream that emphasizes boundary-sensitive detail. Figure 1 presents the overall organization of the Proposed MAFF-Net, in which these two parallel streams are reconciled and combined into a unified representation before decoding.

**Fig. 1.** Overall architecture of the proposed MAFF-Net. A frozen SAM2 encoder produces four multi-scale features, each processed by parallel spatial and frequency pathways whose aligned representations are combined by gated fusion. The three shallower fused features enter the decoder through projected skip connections and the deepest enters as the decoder bottleneck; four deep-supervision heads produce the segmentation outputs, of which the highest-resolution one is the primary prediction, and an auxiliary reconstruction branch operates on the fused features during training.

At a high level, the processing pipeline proceeds as follows. A dermoscopic image is first passed through the frozen encoder, which yields a set of multi-scale feature representations. At each scale, the corresponding feature is routed simultaneously into the spatial stream and the frequency stream, so that contextual and boundary-oriented information are extracted in parallel rather than being entangled. The two streams are subsequently reconciled and combined into a unified representation at each scale. These fused multi-scale representations are propagated to the convolutional decoder, which produces the lesion segmentation map. During training, the auxiliary reconstruction branch operates alongside the decoder to regularize the learned features, and supervision is applied at multiple decoder stages; at inference, only the primary segmentation output is retained.

The architectural components introduced in the fusion pathway of the Proposed MAFF-Net are stated here without implementation detail. First, the Frequency Branch provides an explicit spectral route that emphasizes boundary-related content within each encoder feature. Second, the Feature Alignment Module harmonizes the statistics of the spatial and frequency representations so that they can be combined consistently. Third, the Gated Fusion Module replaces implicit fusion with a learned, lightweight combination of the two aligned streams. The network is additionally trained under Boundary-aware Deep Supervision (BDS) and Edge-weighted Reconstruction (EWR), jointly referred to as Boundary-centric Dual Supervision (BcDS), which direct the optimization toward the lesion contour. The encoder, decoder, and reconstruction branch are preserved from the Original MAFF-Net and are not claimed as contributions of this work.

Taken together, these components constitute a framework that separates spatial and spectral processing, reconciles the two domains before fusion, and reinforces boundary fidelity through BcDS, while continuing to rely on the pretrained backbone. The remaining subsections describe each element of this framework in detail, beginning with the frozen encoder that produces the multi-scale features on which the two streams operate.

III-B. Overall Network Architecture

The Proposed MAFF-Net is organized as a dual-stream encoder–decoder network built upon a pretrained visual foundation backbone. The guiding architectural principle is a clear separation of responsibilities: a pretrained encoder supplies transferable multi-scale representations, two parallel pathways process complementary aspects of these representations, a dedicated stage reconciles and combines the two pathways, and a convolutional decoder produces the segmentation output. This organization preserves the components of the Original MAFF-Net that have already proven effective, namely the frozen encoder, the convolutional decoder, and the auxiliary reconstruction mechanism, and confines the redesign to the pathway responsible for combining spatial and spectral information. The network is therefore best understood as a structured composition of preserved and newly introduced components rather than a monolithic model, with each component assigned a distinct role in the overall processing chain.

The complete architecture is illustrated in Fig. 1, which depicts the end-to-end structure of the Proposed MAFF-Net from the input dermoscopic image to the predicted segmentation map. As shown in the figure, an input image is first encoded into a set of multi-scale feature representations by the frozen encoder. Each of these representations is then dispatched to two parallel pathways: a spatial pathway that retains regional and contextual structure, and a frequency pathway that emphasizes boundary-sensitive content. The two pathways are reconciled by the Feature Alignment Module and merged by the Gated Fusion Module into a single representation at each scale. The resulting fused representations are passed to the convolutional decoder, which aggregates information across scales to yield the segmentation map. An auxiliary reconstruction branch operates alongside the decoder and provides a training-time regularization signal; it is executed on every forward pass, and at inference its reconstruction output is discarded while only the primary segmentation output is retained.

At the level of module organization, the network comprises three functional groups. The first group contains the preserved backbone: the frozen encoder, adopted from the Original MAFF-Net, which provides hierarchical multi-scale features and is not adapted during training. The second group contains the newly introduced fusion pathway, which replaces the single fusion route of the Original MAFF-Net and consists of the Frequency Branch, the Feature Alignment Module, and the Gated Fusion Module. Within this group, the spatial pathway and the frequency pathway operate in parallel on each encoder feature; the Feature Alignment Module brings the two pathways into a common representation; and the Gated Fusion Module combines the aligned representations. The third group contains the preserved decoding and regularization components: the convolutional decoder that reconstructs spatial resolution and produces the prediction, and the auxiliary reconstruction branch that constrains the learned features during training. Supervision of the network is governed by Boundary-centric Dual Supervision, which comprises Boundary-aware Deep Supervision and Edge-weighted Reconstruction.

The interaction between these components defines the information flow of the network. Multi-scale features emerging from the encoder fan out, at every scale, into the spatial and frequency pathways, so that contextual and boundary-oriented information are extracted concurrently rather than being processed in a single entangled route. Each pathway produces its own representation, after which the Feature Alignment Module maps the two representations onto a shared footing so that they can be combined consistently. The Gated Fusion Module then merges the aligned pair into one fused representation per scale, thereby collapsing the two pathways back into a single stream. These fused multi-scale representations propagate into the convolutional decoder, which integrates them from coarse to fine to recover the full-resolution segmentation map. During training, the auxiliary reconstruction branch draws on the fused multi-scale features to encourage stable and informative representations, and supervision is applied at multiple decoder stages so that intermediate predictions are also guided toward the target. The overall flow is thus a parallel-then-merge structure: the representation is split into complementary pathways, reconciled, fused, and finally decoded.

The rationale for this organization follows directly from the difficulties of dermoscopic segmentation. Separating the spatial and frequency pathways provides explicit access to boundary-related spectral content while retaining the contextual structure needed to localize the lesion, which addresses the low contrast and ill-defined boundaries characteristic of these images. Aligning the two pathways before fusion is intended to ensure that representations of different origin are combined on compatible terms, and delegating the combination to a learned gate allows the network to weigh the two domains adaptively rather than committing to a fixed rule. Retaining the pretrained backbone preserves the transferable priors that mitigate the scarcity of annotated dermoscopic data, and retaining the established decoder and reconstruction branch keeps a proven decoding pathway intact, so that the observed behaviour of the network can be attributed to the redesigned fusion pathway and the boundary-centric supervision rather than to changes in the backbone or decoder. This design choice also maintains continuity with the philosophy of the Original MAFF-Net, in which a strong frozen encoder is adapted for the medical domain rather than trained from scratch.

Having established the overall blueprint, the module organization, and the information flow of the Proposed MAFF-Net, the following subsections describe each component in detail. The presentation proceeds through the frozen encoder, the Frequency Branch, the Feature Alignment Module, the Gated Fusion Module, and the decoder with its auxiliary reconstruction branch, and concludes with the training objective that implements Boundary-centric Dual Supervision.

III-C. Frozen Foundation Backbone

Visual foundation models pretrained on large and diverse image collections provide transferable representations that are difficult to obtain from the limited annotated data available in dermoscopy. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) family [9], in particular, has been trained at a scale that yields general-purpose spatial priors, and the Original MAFF-Net demonstrated that such a model can be adapted to skin-lesion segmentation by retaining its image encoder as a fixed feature extractor rather than training a network from scratch. Preserving a pretrained backbone in this manner offers two practical advantages that are especially relevant in the medical setting: it transfers robust representations learned from abundant natural images, and it avoids the risk of overfitting and the substantial cost associated with updating a very large encoder on a comparatively small medical dataset. The Proposed MAFF-Net preserves this design choice without alteration; the SAM2 image encoder [10] is adopted from the Original MAFF-Net and is used as the feature-extraction foundation of the network.

Within the architecture, the backbone serves a single, well-defined role: it maps the input dermoscopic image, standardized to the statistics expected by the encoder, into a set of intermediate feature representations. The encoder parameters are frozen throughout training, meaning that they are not updated by the optimization process and that the encoder is neither modified nor redesigned in this work. Because the backbone is held fixed, the trainable capacity of the network is concentrated entirely in the modules that operate downstream of it, and the representations it produces remain stable and consistent across training. This separation keeps the foundation of the network anchored to the pretrained model while allowing the task-specific behaviour to be learned by the subsequent components. The encoder is therefore used exactly as provided, and none of the contributions claimed in this paper reside within it.

A further property of the backbone that is important for lesion segmentation is that it is hierarchical, producing feature representations at several spatial resolutions and semantic levels rather than a single feature map. The shallower representations retain fine spatial detail and are informative about local structure, whereas the deeper representations encode coarser, more semantic context that describes the lesion as a whole. This range of scales is well suited to dermoscopic images, in which lesions vary considerably in size and in which accurate delineation requires both localization of the overall lesion region and sensitivity to fine boundary structure. By supplying representations that span these scales, the frozen encoder provides the multi-scale information on which scale-adaptive processing depends, and it does so uniformly for every input without any scale-specific tuning.

These multi-scale representations constitute the common input to all downstream components of the Proposed MAFF-Net, and it is at this point that the contributions of the present work begin. Each representation produced by the frozen encoder is subsequently routed into the two parallel pathways, aligned, and combined before decoding, so that the spatial pathway, the Frequency Branch, the Feature Alignment Module, and the Gated Fusion Module all operate on the encoder outputs rather than on the raw image. The backbone thus acts as a shared feature-extraction stage that is preserved from the Original MAFF-Net, and the redesign introduced in this work is deliberately confined to the processing that follows it. Redesigning or fine-tuning the backbone is unnecessary for the goals of this study, because the limitation motivating the proposed modules concerns how spatial and spectral information are combined after extraction, not the quality or availability of the extracted features themselves. Keeping the encoder fixed also ensures that any change in segmentation behaviour can be attributed to the redesigned fusion pathway and the associated supervision rather than to changes in the feature extractor.

In summary, the frozen foundation backbone is preserved from the Original MAFF-Net and serves as the feature-extraction foundation upon which the proposed modules operate; the research contribution of this work begins after feature extraction. Having established the role of the encoder and the nature of the multi-scale representations it provides, the following subsection describes the Frequency Branch, the first of the newly introduced modules, which derives a boundary-sensitive spectral representation from each of these encoder features.

III-D. Frequency Branch for Boundary-Aware Spectral Representation

Accurate boundary delineation is the principal difficulty in dermoscopic segmentation, and boundary-related information is predominantly represented within the higher-frequency components of an image. Repeated downsampling in convolutional encoder hierarchies generally reduces the prominence of fine spatial details while emphasizing higher-level semantic information, so that the finer structure associated with edges tends to become less prominent in the deeper representations relative to the coarser content that describes the lesion interior. This tendency is compounded by the low contrast typical of dermoscopy, where the intensity difference between lesion and surrounding skin is small and spatial gradients are therefore weak and often unreliable indicators of the true boundary. The frequency domain offers a complementary view in which higher-frequency edge structure and lower-frequency background can be separated more explicitly than they are in a single spatial representation. These observations motivate the introduction of the Frequency Branch, which provides the Proposed MAFF-Net with an explicit spectral route dedicated to boundary-sensitive content.

The Frequency Branch is a parameter-free spectral module that is instantiated independently at each encoder scale, so that a dedicated spectral representation is derived at every level of the hierarchical feature set. Its function is to transform an incoming feature representation into the frequency domain, to retain the high-frequency components while suppressing the low-frequency components by means of a radial high-pass filter, and to transform the retained content back into a spatial representation. The filter is governed by a single cutoff that determines the radial boundary between the suppressed low frequencies and the retained high frequencies; this cutoff is a fixed hyperparameter of the module rather than a learned quantity. Because the module contains no trainable parameters, it does not add to the learnable capacity of the network and instead acts as a fixed, interpretable operator whose behaviour is fully determined by the cutoff. The resulting representation emphasizes the higher-frequency structure associated with intensity transitions at lesion boundaries, and constitutes the boundary-aware spectral representation from which the module takes its purpose. As one of the newly introduced components of this work, the Frequency Branch is illustrated schematically in Fig. 2.

**Fig. 2.** Internal processing of the Frequency Branch. An input feature is cast to single precision, transformed by a two-dimensional real FFT, multiplied by a fixed binary radial high-pass mask with cutoff 0.1, and returned to the spatial domain by an inverse real FFT at the original resolution. The module is parameter-free.

The processing performed by the module proceeds along a single, direct path. An encoder feature is first mapped into the frequency domain, where its content is expressed in terms of spatial frequencies. The radial high-pass filter is then applied, suppressing the low-frequency components below the cutoff, which carry the coarse regional appearance, and preserving the high-frequency components that carry edge and fine-detail information. The filtered representation is subsequently returned to the spatial domain, yielding a real-valued output that accentuates the boundary-related structure of the input. A property of this path that is important for the surrounding architecture is that the spatial resolution of the output matches that of the input feature; the module neither downsamples nor upsamples its input, so the spectral representation remains spatially registered with the representation from which it was derived. The operation is applied identically at each scale, differing only in the resolution of the feature on which it acts.

The Frequency Branch is positioned within the dual-stream organization of the Proposed MAFF-Net as the frequency-domain counterpart to the spatial pathway. It receives the same multi-scale features produced by the frozen encoder that are supplied to the spatial pathway, and it operates on them in parallel, so that boundary-sensitive spectral content and contextual spatial content are extracted concurrently from a common source. The output of the Frequency Branch is not consumed directly by the decoder; instead, at each scale it is passed to a Feature Alignment Module, which prepares the spectral representation for combination with the aligned spatial representation. This arrangement keeps the spectral route explicit and separate up to the point of fusion, in contrast to a design in which spatial and spectral information are combined within a single undifferentiated pathway. Because the module preserves resolution and is applied per scale, its output is spatially compatible with the spatial-stream representation at the corresponding level, which is a precondition for the subsequent alignment and fusion stages.

A representation obtained purely from high-frequency spectral content differs in its statistical characteristics from a representation obtained in the spatial domain, and these differences must be reconciled before the two can be combined meaningfully. This reconciliation is the responsibility of the Feature Alignment Module, which is applied to the output of the Frequency Branch and, separately, to the spatial representation, and which is described in the following subsection.

III-E. Feature Alignment Module

The dual-stream design of the Proposed MAFF-Net produces two representations at every encoder scale — one from the spatial pathway and one from the Frequency Branch — that must ultimately be combined into a single representation. These two representations are obtained through different operations and therefore do not share a common statistical footing, and combining them directly would risk allowing one stream to dominate the other or introducing inconsistencies that the fusion stage cannot resolve. It is preferable, before any combination is attempted, to bring the two representations onto comparable terms so that the subsequent fusion operates on quantities of similar scale and distribution. This requirement motivates the Feature Alignment Module, whose role is to reconcile the spatial and frequency representations prior to fusion.

The mismatch between the two streams arises from their distinct origins. The spatial representation is taken directly from the frozen encoder and carries the full range of content that the backbone produces, including the low-frequency and semantic structure that describes the lesion interior, and its per-channel statistics reflect that content. The frequency representation, by contrast, is the output of the Frequency Branch, which suppresses the low-frequency components and retains high-frequency structure; as a consequence it is approximately zero-mean and occupies a different range of values than its spatial counterpart. Differences of this kind, in per-channel offset, scale, and distribution, mean that the two representations are not immediately compatible, and combining them without adjustment would place quantities of dissimilar magnitude on an equal basis. The Feature Alignment Module is introduced specifically to reduce these discrepancies.

The module performs its reconciliation through a compact and interpretable sequence of operations. It first applies an instance normalization, which standardizes the per-channel statistics of a representation on a per-sample basis and thereby removes per-channel offset and scale differences without introducing learnable parameters of its own. The normalized representation is then passed through a learned one-by-one channel projection, followed by a rectified-linear activation, which recombines the channels and applies a nonlinearity while retaining a common channel configuration. The learnable content of the module is therefore confined to this channel projection; the normalization is a fixed statistical operation, and the module does not perform any optimization or learning beyond the projection weights that are updated during training. The effect of this sequence is to place a representation on a normalized, consistently scaled footing suitable for combination with another representation processed in the same manner.

Within the architecture, the module is instantiated separately for each stream at each encoder scale, so that one alignment operates on the spatial encoder feature and another operates on the Frequency Branch output, each with its own parameters. This arrangement means that the spatial and frequency representations are aligned independently but toward a common, comparable form, so that at every scale the two streams arrive at the fusion stage on a harmonized statistical footing and a shared channel configuration. Because the module acts only on the features supplied by the frozen encoder and on the output of the Frequency Branch, it operates entirely downstream of the backbone and does not alter it; the encoder parameters remain fixed, and only the alignment projections are learned. The alignment therefore improves the compatibility of the two streams without modifying the preserved feature extractor, keeping the contributions of this work confined to the processing that follows feature extraction. In this way the module prepares, rather than combines, the two representations: it establishes the conditions under which a meaningful combination can take place, but leaves the combination itself to a subsequent stage.

With the spatial and frequency representations aligned to a comparable footing at each scale, the network is in a position to combine them. The manner of this combination is not fixed in advance; instead, the relative contribution of the two aligned streams is determined adaptively by a learned gating mechanism. This mechanism, the Gated Fusion Module, is described in the following subsection.

III-F. Gated Fusion Module

Once the spatial and frequency representations have been aligned to a comparable footing at each encoder scale, they must be combined into a single representation for decoding. The usefulness of the two streams, however, is not uniform: the relative value of contextual spatial content and boundary-oriented spectral content varies across images and, within a single image, across spatial locations, since the interior of a lesion and the region surrounding its boundary place different demands on the two kinds of information. A combination that treats the two streams identically everywhere cannot account for this variation and may therefore under-use the stream that is locally more informative. This consideration motivates the Gated Fusion Module, whose role is to combine the two aligned representations in a manner that can adjust to the content of the features rather than applying a single fixed rule.

Fixed fusion strategies illustrate the limitation that the module is intended to address. Combining the two aligned representations by simple concatenation followed by a fixed transformation, or by an unweighted summation, applies the same combination at every position and for every input, and consequently cannot adjust the balance between spatial and spectral content according to local image characteristics. In dermoscopic images, where some regions are dominated by homogeneous interior appearance and others by weak or irregular boundaries, a uniform combination cannot reflect the differing contributions that the spatial and frequency streams make in each case. A more suitable combination is one in which the weighting between the two streams is not prescribed in advance but is instead determined from the features being combined, so that the balance can respond to the local content of the representation.

The Gated Fusion Module realizes such a combination through a compact gating operation. At each scale, the aligned spatial and frequency representations are concatenated and passed through a single convolutional operation followed by a sigmoid, which produces a gate whose values lie between zero and one and which varies across both channels and spatial positions. The fused representation is then formed as a convex combination of the two aligned representations, in which the gate weights the spatial representation and its complement weights the frequency representation. Because the gate is bounded and its complement is applied to the other stream, the fused output is a proper interpolation between the two aligned representations rather than an unconstrained mixture, and its values remain within the range spanned by its inputs. The gate is computed from the concatenated representations themselves, so the weighting is data-dependent and adjusts to the features presented at each location; the module does not employ an attention mechanism, and its adaptivity is confined to this learned gating. The Gated Fusion Module is illustrated in Fig. 3.

**Fig. 3.** Feature Alignment and Gated Fusion. The spatial and frequency features are aligned independently by instance normalization, a 1×1 convolution, and a ReLU; the aligned features are then concatenated and passed through a 1×1 convolution and a sigmoid to form a gate g, and the fused feature is the convex combination g·F_s + (1−g)·F_f.

Within the dual-stream organization of the Proposed MAFF-Net, the module is instantiated at each encoder scale and receives, at each scale, the two aligned representations produced by the Feature Alignment Module. It is therefore the stage at which the two parallel streams are brought back together: for every scale it consumes an aligned spatial representation and an aligned frequency representation and returns a single fused representation. These fused representations constitute the multi-scale features that the remainder of the network operates on; the deepest fused representation enters the decoder directly as its bottleneck input, while the three shallower fused representations are carried to the decoder through projected skip connections, so that the fused output of each scale contributes to the reconstruction of the segmentation map at the corresponding resolution. In this way the Gated Fusion Module occupies the position between alignment and decoding, converting the two aligned streams into the unified multi-scale representation on which the decoder depends, and it does so without altering the preserved encoder or decoder.

With the two aligned streams combined into fused multi-scale representations, the network is ready to reconstruct the segmentation map from these representations. The decoding of the fused features, together with the auxiliary reconstruction branch that accompanies it, both of which are preserved from the Original MAFF-Net, is described in the following subsection.

III-G. Decoder and Reconstruction Pathway

Once the Gated Fusion Module has produced a fused representation at each encoder scale, a decoding stage is required to transform this set of fused multi-scale representations into a full-resolution segmentation prediction. The decoder is the final stage of the Proposed MAFF-Net, where the fused features are progressively resolved into a dense output at the resolution of the input image. The decoding pathway is built on the decoding design of the Original MAFF-Net, and it is not among the scientific contributions of this work; its function here is to convert the outputs of the proposed fusion pathway into a segmentation map. This subsection describes how the fused representations are decoded and how an accompanying reconstruction branch operates on the same representations, and it distinguishes the adjustments made to this pathway from the contributions introduced in the preceding subsections.

Decoding proceeds hierarchically, from the coarsest fused representation to the finest. Beginning with the fused representation at the deepest scale, the decoder increases spatial resolution stage by stage; at each stage an upsampled higher-level feature is combined with a skip connection carrying information from the corresponding shallower scale, and the combined feature is refined before being passed to the next stage. The finest stage refines the representation without a skip connection to complete the recovery of full resolution. Predictions are produced at multiple decoder stages, and the prediction obtained at the highest resolution constitutes the segmentation output of the network, while the predictions at the coarser stages provide additional multi-scale outputs. This coarse-to-fine organization allows the decoder to recover the overall lesion region at the deeper stages and to sharpen the prediction toward the boundary as resolution increases.

The fused representations produced by the Gated Fusion Module differ from the features that a conventional decoder would ordinarily receive, and the decoding pathway is therefore adjusted so that it can accept them. In the Proposed MAFF-Net, the skip connections that enter the decoder are formed by applying a projection to the fused representations before they are combined with the upsampled decoder features, so that the outputs of the dual-stream fusion are routed into the decoder in a consistent form. This projection and the associated routing are architectural adaptations required to accommodate the fused multi-scale representations; they are engineering adjustments that make the fusion pathway and the decoder compatible, and they are not presented as scientific contributions. The research contributions of this work remain the Frequency Branch, the Feature Alignment Module, and the Gated Fusion Module described in the preceding subsections, together with the supervision strategy described next; the decoding pathway is adapted around these contributions rather than being one of them.

Alongside the segmentation decoder, the Proposed MAFF-Net includes an auxiliary reconstruction branch that operates on the same fused multi-scale representations and produces an image-sized reconstruction of the input. This branch reconstructs an output at the resolution of the input image and provides an auxiliary reconstruction signal that supports training by encouraging the fused representations to retain information about the input. As with the decoder, the reconstruction branch follows the reconstruction design of the Original MAFF-Net and is adapted here to consume the fused representations produced by the Gated Fusion Module rather than being reproduced unchanged; this adaptation, too, is an engineering adjustment and is not claimed as a contribution. The segmentation decoder and the reconstruction branch thus draw on the same fused representations for two purposes: the decoder yields the lesion prediction, and the reconstruction branch yields an auxiliary output that regularizes the shared representations during training.

The decoder and the reconstruction branch together determine the outputs of the network — the multi-scale segmentation predictions and the auxiliary reconstruction — but they do not by themselves specify how those outputs are supervised. The manner in which the multi-scale predictions and the reconstruction are used during training, and in particular the boundary-centric strategy that forms part of the training objective of this work, is the subject of the following subsection on Boundary-centric Dual Supervision.

III-H. Boundary-centric Dual Supervision

The primary training signal for the Proposed MAFF-Net is a region-based segmentation objective that measures the agreement between the predicted and reference lesion regions. Such region objectives are effective at recovering the overall extent of a lesion, but they are comparatively insensitive to errors that are confined to the boundary, because boundary pixels are few relative to the lesion interior and a small displacement of the contour changes the region-overlap measure only marginally. This limitation is particularly consequential in dermoscopy, where lesion margins are frequently diffuse, gradual, or irregular, and where the clinical value of a segmentation depends heavily on the fidelity of the delineated boundary. A region objective alone therefore provides limited pressure to correct precisely the kind of boundary errors that matter most for this task. To address this without displacing the region objective, the Proposed MAFF-Net augments it with supervision that explicitly targets the lesion contour, referred to as Boundary-centric Dual Supervision. BcDS supplements, rather than replaces, the primary region segmentation objective, and is composed of two complementary pathways: Boundary-aware Deep Supervision applied to the segmentation outputs, and Edge-weighted Reconstruction applied to the reconstruction branch.

Boundary-aware Deep Supervision acts on the predictions produced by the decoder. As described in the preceding subsection, the decoder emits predictions at multiple stages under deep supervision; BDS supervises these predictions by comparing their boundary structure with the boundary structure of the ground-truth mask, so that discrepancies located at the lesion contour are penalized directly. In contrast to the region objective, which compares predicted and reference areas, BDS compares predicted and reference edges, and therefore directs learning specifically toward accurate margins rather than toward overall region agreement. Because it is applied to the same multi-scale predictions that the region objective supervises, BDS adds a contour-oriented signal at each supervised decoder stage while leaving the region objective intact. In this way BDS provides an explicit, boundary-focused complement to the primary segmentation loss.

Edge-weighted Reconstruction acts on the auxiliary reconstruction branch. The reconstruction branch produces an image-sized reconstruction of the input, which serves during training as a regularizing signal on the shared fused representations. EWR shapes this signal so that the reconstruction discrepancy is emphasized in the regions surrounding the lesion boundary and de-emphasized in the comparatively uniform interior, turning an otherwise region-agnostic reconstruction objective into a boundary-emphasized one. By concentrating the reconstruction pressure along the margins, EWR encourages the fused representations on which both the decoder and the reconstruction branch depend to retain the boundary-related information that the segmentation prediction requires. EWR thus reinforces boundary fidelity indirectly, through the representations, rather than by acting on the segmentation prediction itself.

The two pathways are complementary because they impose a shared boundary focus through different outputs of the network. BDS operates on the segmentation predictions and shapes the predicted margins directly, whereas EWR operates on the reconstruction branch and reinforces boundary information within the shared representations that feed the decoder. Neither pathway duplicates the other: one supervises what the network predicts at the contour, while the other supervises how well the underlying representations preserve boundary content. Taken together, and applied alongside the primary region objective, these two boundary-focused pathways constitute the dual character of Boundary-centric Dual Supervision. Both pathways are auxiliary to, and coherent with, the region segmentation objective; they are intended to sharpen boundary delineation while the region objective continues to govern the recovery of the overall lesion region.

Boundary-centric Dual Supervision therefore defines how the multi-scale segmentation predictions and the auxiliary reconstruction are supervised with a common emphasis on the lesion boundary, while the region objective remains the primary training signal. The precise formulation of the region objective, of the two boundary-focused supervision terms, and of the manner in which all of these are combined into a single training objective is presented in the following subsection.

III-I. Training Objective / Loss Formulation

The Proposed MAFF-Net is trained by minimizing a single composite objective that combines a primary region-segmentation term with the two auxiliary boundary-focused terms of Boundary-centric Dual Supervision introduced in the previous subsection. Let $x$ denote the input dermoscopic image with $C$ channels, $y \in \{0,1\}^{H \times W}$ the binary ground-truth lesion mask, and $p_i$, for $i = 1,\dots,N$ with $N = 4$, the segmentation predictions (as logits) produced at the decoder stages under deep supervision. The logistic sigmoid is written $\sigma(\cdot)$, and sums indexed by $u$ run over spatial locations. The region term supervises the predicted lesion area, whereas the two Boundary-centric Dual Supervision terms supervise the lesion boundary; the region term is the primary training signal and the boundary terms are auxiliary.

The region term is built from two standard segmentation losses. The soft Dice loss measures the overlap between a predicted probability map and the ground-truth mask,
$$
L_{\mathrm{Dice}}(p,y) = 1 - \frac{2\sum_{u}\sigma(p)_u\,y_u + s}{\sum_{u}\sigma(p)_u + \sum_{u}y_u + s}, \tag{1}
$$
where $s$ is a small smoothing constant that keeps the expression well defined when the mask or prediction is empty. A value of $L_{\mathrm{Dice}}$ near zero indicates strong overlap. The binary cross-entropy loss, applied to the logits with a positive-class weight $\omega$, measures per-pixel classification agreement,
$$
L_{\mathrm{BCE}}(p,y) = -\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{u \in \Omega}\Big[\omega\,y_u\log\sigma(p)_u + (1-y_u)\log\big(1-\sigma(p)_u\big)\Big], \tag{2}
$$
where $\Omega$ is the set of spatial locations and $\omega$ up-weights lesion (positive) pixels relative to background pixels to counteract class imbalance.

The region segmentation objective aggregates these two losses over the deep-supervision predictions. Each of the $N$ decoder predictions is supervised with the sum of the Dice and cross-entropy losses, and the results are averaged uniformly across the predictions,
$$
L_{\mathrm{seg}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[L_{\mathrm{Dice}}(p_i,y) + L_{\mathrm{BCE}}(p_i,y)\Big]. \tag{3}
$$
The uniform average in Eq. (3) is the deep-supervision aggregation used for the region term: every prediction contributes equally, so the region objective supervises the coarse and fine decoder stages with the same weight.

Boundary-aware Deep Supervision compares the boundary structure of the predictions with that of the ground truth. Boundaries are extracted by an edge operator $S(\cdot)$ that applies the horizontal and vertical Sobel filters $G_x$ and $G_y$, forms the gradient magnitude, and bounds it to the unit interval,
$$
S(m) = \mathrm{clamp}\!\Big(\sqrt{(G_x * m)^2 + (G_y * m)^2 + \varepsilon},\,0,\,1\Big), \tag{4a}
$$
where $*$ denotes convolution and $\varepsilon$ is a small constant that keeps the gradient of the square root finite in flat regions. The Boundary-aware Deep Supervision loss is then the weighted average, over the deep-supervision predictions, of the discrepancy between the predicted and ground-truth edge maps,
$$
L_{\mathrm{BDS}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i}w_i}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \,\big\lVert\, S\big(\tilde{\sigma}(p_i)\big) - S(y)\,\big\rVert_1, \tag{4b}
$$
where $\tilde{\sigma}(p_i)$ is a smoothed sigmoid prediction and $w_i$ are per-prediction weights. In contrast to the uniform aggregation of Eq. (3), Eq. (4b) uses the weights $w_i$, so the finest predictions dominate the boundary term while the coarsest contributes only marginally.

The boundary term is introduced gradually rather than from the first iteration. Its weight follows a linear warm-up schedule,
$$
\lambda_{B}(t) = \lambda_{\mathrm{bds}}\cdot\min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{t}{T_{\mathrm{warm}}}\Big), \tag{5}
$$
where $t$ is the current epoch index, $T_{\mathrm{warm}}$ is the warm-up length, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{bds}}$ is the maximum boundary weight. According to Eq. (5), the Boundary-aware Deep Supervision weight increases from zero to $\lambda_{\mathrm{bds}}$ over the first $T_{\mathrm{warm}}$ epochs and remains constant thereafter, so the region objective governs the early training and the boundary term is phased in once the predictions have stabilized.

Edge-weighted Reconstruction supervises the auxiliary reconstruction branch with a boundary-emphasized reconstruction error. A spatial weight map is derived from the ground-truth boundary, $M = 1 + \alpha\,B(y)$, where $B(y)$ is a boundary band obtained from $S(y)$ and $\alpha \ge 0$ controls the emphasis placed on boundary regions. Writing $r$ for the reconstruction mapped to the range of the input image, the loss is a normalized weighted mean-squared error,
$$
L_{\mathrm{EWR}} = \frac{\sum_{u} M_u\,(r_u - x_u)^2}{C\sum_{u} M_u}. \tag{6}
$$
Because $M$ exceeds unity along the lesion boundary and equals unity elsewhere, Eq. (6) concentrates the reconstruction pressure on boundary regions while normalizing by the total weight and the number of channels $C$.

The complete training objective is the weighted sum of the region term and the two boundary terms,
$$
L = L_{\mathrm{seg}} + \lambda_{B}(t)\,L_{\mathrm{BDS}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{ewr}}\,L_{\mathrm{EWR}}, \tag{7}
$$
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{ewr}}$ is the reconstruction weight. In the implemented configuration, the region objective carries unit weight and is therefore the dominant term, while the boundary terms are auxiliary, with $\lambda_{\mathrm{bds}} = 0.05$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{ewr}} = 0.05$; the remaining constants are $\omega = 2$, $\alpha = 3$, $T_{\mathrm{warm}} = 5$, and per-prediction weights $w = (1, 1, 1, 0.01)$, with $s$ and $\varepsilon$ set to small fixed values. Equation (7) thus realizes the supervision framework of the preceding subsection: a primary region objective supplemented by the boundary-focused terms of Boundary-centric Dual Supervision.

The objective in Eq. (7) is minimized over the trainable parameters of the network — the fusion-pathway modules, the decoder, the segmentation heads, and the auxiliary reconstruction branch — while the encoder remains frozen. Optimization uses the AdamW optimizer with decoupled weight decay, a learning rate that is decayed linearly toward zero over the course of training, and gradient-norm clipping; training is performed under mixed-precision autocasting, and the model used for evaluation is the one achieving the best validation Dice score. Having specified the training objective, the following section describes the datasets, implementation details, and evaluation protocol used in the experiments.

# IV. Experimental Setup

IV-A. Datasets

The proposed framework is evaluated on three publicly available dermoscopic skin-lesion segmentation benchmarks released through the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC): ISIC 2016 [20], ISIC 2017 [21], and ISIC 2018 [22]. Each benchmark provides dermoscopic images of pigmented skin lesions together with expert-annotated binary masks that delineate the lesion region from the surrounding skin. The three datasets differ in scale and in the diversity of lesion appearance, and together they span the range of imaging conditions — low lesion-to-skin contrast, diffuse or irregular boundaries, and occlusion by hair and other structures — that motivate the boundary-oriented design described in Section III. Evaluating across all three benchmarks allows the behaviour of the network to be assessed under progressively larger and more heterogeneous data rather than on a single collection.

Each dataset is partitioned into disjoint training, validation, and test subsets, and these partitions are held fixed throughout all experiments so that no image contributes to more than one subset. For ISIC 2016 the partition comprises 895 training, 192 validation, and 192 test images; for ISIC 2017, 1600 training, 200 validation, and 200 test images; and for ISIC 2018, 1825 training, 377 validation, and 392 test images. The validation subset is used exclusively for model selection, and the test subset is reserved for final evaluation and is never consulted during training or checkpoint selection. This separation ensures that all reported comparisons are made on data unseen during optimisation.

All images and masks undergo an identical preprocessing procedure prior to training and evaluation. Each dermoscopic image is read as a three-channel RGB image and each mask as a single-channel grayscale image, and both are resized to a fixed spatial resolution of 384×384 pixels, with bilinear interpolation used for the images and nearest-neighbour interpolation for the masks; the latter preserves the binary character of the annotations. Each resized mask is binarised at a threshold of one half, so that every pixel is assigned unambiguously to the lesion or to the background. During training, and only on the training subset, standard geometric and photometric data augmentation is applied to improve robustness to the variability of dermoscopic acquisition: random horizontal and vertical flips, random rotation within a range of thirty degrees, an occasional random affine transformation combining limited rotation, translation, scaling, and shear, and a mild jitter of brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue. The validation and test images are processed without augmentation. Standardisation of the input intensities to the statistics expected by the frozen encoder is performed within the network, as described in Section III-C, rather than as a dataset-level operation, so that every model receives inputs on a common footing.

IV-B. Implementation Details

All networks operate on inputs of 384×384 pixels and are trained under a single, consistent configuration, so that differences in behaviour can be attributed to the architectures rather than to the training regime. Optimisation uses the AdamW optimiser with decoupled weight decay, an initial learning rate of $1\times10^{-3}$, and a weight decay of $1\times10^{-2}$. The learning rate is annealed by a linear schedule that decays it from its initial value to zero over the course of training, and a mini-batch size of two images is used throughout. Training is performed under mixed-precision automatic casting in the bfloat16 format, whose full-range exponent removes the need for gradient loss scaling, and gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of unity at each step to guard against occasional large updates. Each model is trained for up to forty epochs; the convolutional baseline networks are additionally governed by early stopping on the validation Dice score, with a patience of ten epochs, whereas the models of the MAFF-Net family complete the full schedule.

The training objective differs between the two groups of models in accordance with their design. The MAFF-Net family — the Proposed MAFF-Net together with the Restored MAFF-Net and the Paper-faithful Reconstruction used as reference points — is trained with the composite objective of Boundary-centric Dual Supervision defined in Section III-I, which augments a region-based deep-supervision term with the boundary-focused supervision applied to the segmentation heads and the reconstruction branch. The convolutional baselines are trained with a standard region objective that combines the soft Dice loss with a class-weighted binary cross-entropy, the positive class being up-weighted to counteract the imbalance between lesion and background pixels. In every case the pretrained SAM2 (Hiera-Large) image encoder [10] is retained as a frozen feature extractor: its parameters are not updated by the optimiser, and the trainable capacity of the network is confined to the modules that operate downstream of it, consistent with the role of the backbone established in Section III-C.

All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 2000 Ada Generation graphics processing unit. The software environment comprises Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.7.1 built against CUDA 11.8, on the Windows operating system. The boundary-based metrics introduced below are computed with the MedPy library (version 0.5.2, which relies on SimpleITK) [23] together with a direct implementation of the Boundary IoU measure; these are used solely for evaluation and form no part of the training pipeline.

IV-C. Evaluation Metrics

Segmentation quality is assessed with two complementary families of metrics. The first family measures the region overlap between the predicted and reference lesion regions, and the second measures the fidelity of the predicted lesion boundary. Reporting both is essential for this study because the contributions of Section III are directed specifically at the lesion contour, and, as argued in Section III-H, region-overlap measures are comparatively insensitive to errors confined to the boundary; boundary-based metrics are therefore required to characterise the very behaviour the method is designed to improve. For all metrics, predictions are obtained by applying the logistic sigmoid to the network output and thresholding at one half, using the primary segmentation head of the deep-supervision hierarchy, and each metric is aggregated over the test set as the mean across evaluation batches weighted by the number of images in each batch. A small constant is added to the denominators to keep every expression well defined when a region is empty.

The region-based metrics are derived from the per-pixel counts of true positives $\mathrm{TP}$, false positives $\mathrm{FP}$, true negatives $\mathrm{TN}$, and false negatives $\mathrm{FN}$, computed between the thresholded prediction and the binary reference. The pixel accuracy measures the overall proportion of correctly classified pixels,
$$
\mathrm{Accuracy} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN}}, \tag{8}
$$
the Dice similarity coefficient measures the overlap between the predicted and reference lesion regions,
$$
\mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{2\,\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}, \tag{9}
$$
and the Intersection-over-Union (IoU), or Jaccard index, of the lesion region provides a related but stricter overlap measure,
$$
\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}. \tag{10}
$$
The sensitivity, equivalently the recall, quantifies the proportion of lesion pixels that are correctly recovered, while the specificity quantifies the proportion of background pixels correctly identified,
$$
\mathrm{Sensitivity} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
\mathrm{Specificity} = \frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}}. \tag{11}
$$
The IoU is reported for the lesion (foreground) class; the accuracy, being dominated by the large background region typical of dermoscopic images, is reported for completeness rather than as a primary discriminator.

The boundary-based metrics operate on the contours of the predicted and reference regions. Let $\partial P$ and $\partial G$ denote the sets of boundary pixels of the prediction and the ground truth, and for a point $a$ and a set $S$ let $d(a,S)=\min_{s\in S}\lVert a-s\rVert$ be the Euclidean distance from $a$ to its nearest point in $S$. The average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) is the mean of the nearest-neighbour distances taken in both directions between the two contours,
$$
\mathrm{ASSD} = \frac{\sum_{p\in\partial P} d(p,\partial G) + \sum_{g\in\partial G} d(g,\partial P)}{\lvert\partial P\rvert + \lvert\partial G\rvert}, \tag{12}
$$
and the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) is the 95th percentile of the same set of symmetric point-to-contour distances, a robust variant of the Hausdorff distance that discounts the most extreme outliers,
$$
\mathrm{HD95} = \mathrm{P}_{95}\big(\{\, d(p,\partial G) : p\in\partial P\,\} \cup \{\, d(g,\partial P) : g\in\partial G\,\}\big). \tag{13}
$$
Both ASSD and HD95 are distance measures, expressed in pixels of the 384×384 evaluation grid, for which smaller values indicate closer agreement between the predicted and reference contours; because the images are resized from heterogeneous native resolutions and no physical pixel spacing is retained, these distances are reported in pixel units rather than in millimetres. The Boundary IoU complements them with an overlap measure restricted to a narrow band around the contour: following the standard formulation [12], a boundary band is obtained for each mask by subtracting its morphological erosion from the mask, the width of the band being set to two per cent of the image diagonal, and the intersection-over-union is then computed between the two boundary bands. Unlike the region IoU, the Boundary IoU is sensitive to displacements of the contour even when the overall region overlap is large, and larger values indicate better boundary agreement. Taken together, the region-based metrics characterise how well each method recovers the overall lesion extent, and the boundary-based metrics characterise how faithfully it recovers the lesion margin.

IV-D. Experimental Protocol

The Proposed MAFF-Net is compared against four representative segmentation networks that are trained and evaluated within the same repository under the protocol described above: the U-Net, the Attention U-Net, the UNet++, and the TransUNet. These baselines span classical encoder–decoder, attention-gated, nested, and transformer-hybrid designs, and they are trained on the identical data partitions, at the identical input resolution, and with the identical batch size as the proposed model, differing only in the training objective appropriate to each family of models as noted in Section IV-B. The Restored MAFF-Net and the Paper-faithful Reconstruction are evaluated under the same protocol and serve as reference points within the MAFF-Net family rather than as external baselines.

A single canonical evaluation protocol is applied uniformly to every model. For each training run the checkpoint achieving the highest Dice score on the validation subset is retained, and this best-validation checkpoint alone is carried forward to evaluation; no test-set information influences its selection. Each retained checkpoint is then evaluated once on the held-out test subset of the corresponding dataset, and the region-based and boundary-based metrics of Section IV-C are computed from the same set of predictions, using the same thresholding and the same aggregation, so that all models are compared on a common basis. Evaluating every model, baseline and proposed alike, with this shared procedure ensures that the reported differences reflect the models rather than differences in measurement.

The architectural contributions of the Proposed MAFF-Net are examined through a component ablation conducted on the ISIC 2018 dataset, the largest and most heterogeneous of the three benchmarks. The ablation follows a leave-one-out design in which the complete Proposed MAFF-Net serves as the reference configuration and three further configurations are obtained by disabling exactly one architectural component at a time: the Frequency Branch, the Feature Alignment Module, and the Gated Fusion Module. In each ablated configuration the disabled component is replaced by a shape-preserving substitute that leaves the remainder of the network and its tensor dimensions unchanged — the Frequency Branch and the Feature Alignment Module are replaced by identity mappings, and the learned gate of the Gated Fusion Module is replaced by a fixed, unweighted mean of the two streams — so that the effect of each component can be isolated. Every ablated configuration is trained with the identical configuration used for the Proposed MAFF-Net and is evaluated with the same canonical protocol and the same region-based and boundary-based metrics, and the reference configuration reuses the existing Proposed MAFF-Net checkpoint without retraining. The frozen encoder, the decoder, and the reconstruction branch are held fixed across all ablated configurations, so that the study measures only the contributions of the components introduced in this work.

The following section reports the outcome of these experiments, presenting first the comparison of the Proposed MAFF-Net with the baseline networks under the region-based and boundary-based metrics, and then the component ablation on ISIC 2018.

A contour displaced by a few pixels alters the region overlap only marginally, so a model may attain a high overlap score while still reproducing the margin imprecisely. Measures defined directly on the contour are therefore required in order to characterise this aspect of segmentation quality. V-D. Qualitative Analysis

The quantitative measures reported above summarise agreement over entire test subsets and do not indicate the manner in which the predicted contours depart from the reference annotations. Figure 4 accordingly presents representative segmentation results on ISIC 2018 for the baseline networks and for the members of the MAFF-Net family. The same images and the same reference annotations are used for every method, so that the predictions may be compared directly. The cases shown are illustrative examples selected to span the difficulties characteristic of dermoscopic imaging, and they are intended to complement rather than to substantiate the quantitative findings.

A characteristic common to all evaluated methods concerns the fine structure of the contour. The reference annotations exhibit pixel-level serration along the lesion margin, whereas the predictions of every method, baseline and MAFF-Net variant alike, are comparatively smooth at that scale. None of the evaluated models reproduces the finest irregularities of the manual delineation, and the differences observed between methods therefore concern the coarser shape of the contour rather than its fine detail. This observation applies uniformly and should be borne in mind when the visual results are assessed.

For lesions of low contrast and diffuse margin, in which the pigmented region fades gradually into the surrounding skin, the methods differ chiefly in the degree to which the predicted contour follows the outline of the reference. The baseline networks recover the overall extent of such lesions, but their contours tend towards rounded and approximately convex forms. This tendency is most evident in the predictions of the TransUNet, which reproduces the lesion as a compact convex region and does not follow the concavities present in the reference outline. The predictions of the Restored and Proposed MAFF-Net follow the asymmetric form of the reference more closely in these cases, reproducing indentations along the margin and the elongation of the lesion towards one side, although, as noted above, their contours remain smoother than the annotation at the finest scale.

The methods also differ in the internal consistency of the predicted regions. The predictions of the convolutional baselines contain, in several of the cases examined, small unsegmented cavities within the lesion interior and isolated fragments detached from the principal region along the margin, giving the boundary a broken appearance in places. The predictions of the MAFF-Net family in the same cases form closed regions without interior cavities, and the lesion is returned as a single connected component with a continuous margin. The contours produced by the MAFF-Net variants are correspondingly more regular, in the sense of exhibiting fewer isolated fragments and fewer discontinuities, rather than in the sense of being more detailed.

The cases containing structures adjacent to the target lesion are the most discriminating of those examined. In one such case the dermoscopic image is traversed by dense hair and contains a second, darker pigmented structure near the image border which the reference annotation excludes. The U-Net, the UNet++, and the Attention U-Net each segment this adjacent structure as an additional region, and the TransUNet segments it and further connects it to the target lesion, returning the two as one continuous region. The Restored MAFF-Net and the Proposed MAFF-Net confine the segmentation to the target lesion and return its margin as a closed contour; a residual response to the adjacent structure remains in both, larger in the prediction of the Restored MAFF-Net and reduced to a small isolated fragment in that of the Proposed MAFF-Net. In the same case the dense hair overlying the lesion does not visibly fragment the contours produced by the MAFF-Net variants, whereas the margins produced by several of the baselines are locally irregular in the regions the hair traverses.

These observations are subject to clear limitations. They rest on a small number of saved examples per model, drawn from the validation split rather than from the test subsets on which the quantitative results were computed, and they derive from single training runs. They are consequently illustrative of the behaviour of the models on the cases shown and are not a representative sample, nor do they constitute independent evidence of the differences reported in Sections V-A to V-C. The visual comparison is presented for interpretive value only, and the quantitative measures remain the basis for the comparisons drawn in this work.

# VI. Discussion

VI-A. Interpretation of the Component-wise Observations

The ablation reported in Section V-C establishes that disabling any one of the three components introduced in this work degrades all three boundary measures simultaneously, while the region measures remain within a narrow band across the configurations and are not maximised by the complete model. These are marginal effects measured within the Proposed model, each ablated configuration differing from the complete model by a single disabled component; they characterise the role of each component within that model and are not comparisons with the other members of the MAFF-Net family. The experiments determine that this association exists; they do not, by themselves, identify the mechanism responsible for it, since no analysis of the intermediate representations was undertaken. The accounts offered below are therefore interpretations consistent with the design described in Section III and with the observed behaviour, and they are advanced as such rather than as demonstrated explanations.

The Frequency Branch applies a fixed radial high-pass filter to each encoder feature and returns the retained content to the spatial domain. Intensity transitions at the lesion margin are among the structures represented in the higher-frequency portion of the spectrum, and a plausible reading of the ablation is that retaining an explicit route for this content preserves margin-associated information that would otherwise be carried in a single representation together with the lower-frequency content describing the comparatively uniform lesion interior. One aspect of this comparison is unusually clean and merits emphasis: the module contains no trainable parameters, so replacing it with an identity mapping leaves the trainable parameter count of the network unchanged. The degradation in the boundary measures that follows its removal therefore cannot be attributed to a reduction in model capacity; in so far as this single-run difference reflects a genuine effect rather than run-to-run variation, it is attributable to the spectral operation itself. This does not establish which spectral content is responsible, only that the operation, and not the capacity, would account for such a difference.

Removing the Feature Alignment Module produced the largest degradation in the average symmetric surface distance and in the Boundary IoU of the three ablations. The two streams reaching the fusion stage are of different provenance: the spatial stream carries the full content of the encoder feature, whereas the frequency stream, having had its low-frequency components suppressed, is approximately zero-mean and occupies a different range of values. A reasonable interpretation is that combining representations of dissimilar offset and scale permits one stream to dominate the combination, and that normalising both onto a common footing before the gate is computed allows the subsequent weighting to reflect the content of the two streams rather than their relative magnitudes. This interpretation carries an important qualification. The module comprises a normalisation together with a learned projection and a nonlinearity, and disabling it removes all three; the ablated configuration therefore has slightly fewer trainable parameters than the complete model. The observed degradation cannot be separated, on the present evidence, into the contribution of the normalisation and that of the reduced capacity.

Replacing the Gated Fusion Module with a fixed unweighted mean produced the largest increase in the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance of the three ablations, while producing the smallest changes in the average symmetric surface distance and the Boundary IoU. A fixed mean applies the same weighting to the two streams at every position and for every input, whereas the gate is computed from the streams themselves and may vary across channels and spatial positions. If the relative usefulness of contextual and margin-associated content differs between the lesion interior and the neighbourhood of the margin, a position-dependent weighting is able to reflect that difference where a fixed rule cannot. That the effect of this module is most pronounced on the 95th-percentile distance, a measure governed by the larger contour deviations, and least pronounced on the mean surface distance may indicate that its influence is concentrated on the more severe departures from the reference contour rather than on the typical ones; this remains a conjecture that the present experiments do not test. Here too the ablated configuration has marginally fewer trainable parameters than the complete model.

VI-B. Boundary Fidelity versus Region Overlap

That the improvements associated with these components appear in the boundary measures and not in the Dice coefficient follows from a geometric property of the measures themselves rather than from any peculiarity of the models. Region overlap is an area-based quantity, and the neighbourhood of the contour occupies a small fraction of the area of a compact lesion. Displacing a contour by a few pixels alters the overlapping area only slightly, and the resulting change in the Dice coefficient is correspondingly small; the same displacement enters a contour-distance measure directly and in full. A modification that acts principally on the placement of the margin may therefore leave region overlap almost unchanged while producing a measurable change in the boundary measures. The observations of Section V-C are consistent with this account: the region measures varied within a narrow band across the four configurations, and the complete model did not record the highest value on any of them, whereas the boundary measures separated the configurations consistently.

Two consequences follow for the interpretation of this work. The first is that the contribution of the three components is properly characterised as an increase in the fidelity of the predicted contour relative to the configurations in which those components are disabled, and not as an increase in region-overlap performance; the evidence does not support the latter and no such claim is made. This characterisation is internal to the Proposed model, and is not a claim of superior contour fidelity relative to the other members of the MAFF-Net family, among which, as recorded in Section VI-D, the Restored MAFF-Net attains the closer agreement with the reference contours. The second is that region-overlap measures alone are insufficient to characterise the behaviour of methods designed to act on the lesion margin, a point for which Section V-B provides independent support in the disagreement between the orderings induced by the two families of measures. This second observation is methodological rather than architectural; it follows from the geometric property of the measures noted above rather than from any feature of the models examined, and is therefore expected to bear on the evaluation of other methods intended to act on the object boundary. Both consequences should nonetheless be weighed against the magnitudes involved, which are small in relation to the per-image dispersion of the distance measures.

VI-C. Retaining the Frozen Foundation Backbone

Retaining the pretrained encoder as a fixed feature extractor carries several advantages in this setting. The representations it provides are learned from a volume and diversity of data that the available dermoscopic collections do not approach, which is material given the size of the datasets employed here. Because the encoder is not updated, the trainable capacity of the network is concentrated in the modules that operate downstream of it and constitutes a small fraction of the total parameter count, which reduces the number of parameters that must be estimated from the comparatively limited annotated data available.

A further advantage is methodological and bears directly on the interpretation of the results. Because the same frozen encoder is used by the Proposed MAFF-Net, by the Restored MAFF-Net, and by every ablated configuration, the features entering the fusion pathway are identical across those configurations. Differences in their behaviour can accordingly be attributed to the modules under study rather than to differences in feature extraction, which is what permits the ablation of Section V-C to be read as an assessment of the components themselves. An architecture in which the backbone were also adapted would not support that inference as directly. The same reasoning applies to the comparison between the Proposed MAFF-Net and the Restored MAFF-Net: because the two share the frozen encoder and differ only in the fusion pathway, the difference between them reported in Sections V-A and V-B is attributable to the fusion design rather than to feature extraction. On these benchmarks the dual-stream fusion does not surpass the multi-scale perception adaptation and frequency-guided feature fusion of the Original MAFF-Net, and the dual-stream reformulation is accordingly to be understood as an alternative fusion design that is examined and analysed here rather than one demonstrated to improve upon the original.

These advantages should not be overstated. The present experiments do not include a configuration in which the encoder is fine-tuned, and the comparative merits of freezing and fine-tuning are therefore not assessed here; the rationale for freezing rests on the considerations above rather than on evidence produced by this study. It should further be recalled, as noted in Section V-A, that the Paper-faithful Reconstruction was trained without pretrained encoder weights, so the comparison between it and the remaining members of the family does not isolate the effect of the pretrained backbone.

VI-D. Limitations

Several limitations qualify the findings of this work and delimit the conclusions that may properly be drawn from them.

Each configuration reported in this study was trained once, with a single experimental seed, and no configuration was repeated. The differences between closely ranked models, and in particular those separating the members of the MAFF-Net family and the four ablated configurations, are small in relation to the per-image dispersion of the measures concerned. It is consequently not possible, on the present evidence, to distinguish these differences from the variation that would arise between repeated runs of the same configuration. Related to this, no statistical analysis was performed: no confidence intervals were estimated and no hypothesis tests were conducted, and no claim of statistical significance is made anywhere in this work. Repeated runs across several seeds, accompanied by an appropriate interval estimate, would be required before the smaller differences reported here could be regarded as established.

The supervision strategy described in Section III-H was active in every configuration reported in this study, and no ablation of its constituent terms was performed. The contribution of the boundary-focused supervision terms to the results is therefore unmeasured, and no claim is advanced regarding their individual efficacy. The component ablation of Section V-C addresses the three architectural modules alone, and was moreover conducted on a single dataset, so the consistency of its findings across the other two benchmarks is not established.

The evaluation is confined to three dermoscopic benchmarks drawn from the same challenge series. All images derive from a single imaging modality and a common annotation protocol, and the generality of the findings to other modalities, to other acquisition devices, or to patient populations differing from those represented in these collections has not been assessed. The comparison is further restricted to models trained and evaluated within this study; no comparison is drawn against results reported in the literature, and several methods discussed in Section II were not implemented here.

Finally, the relative standing of the members of the MAFF-Net family should be stated plainly. The Restored MAFF-Net records the higher value on the majority of the measures reported in Sections V-A and V-B, including the Dice coefficient on all three datasets and the two distance measures on all three datasets. The evidence assembled here therefore does not establish the proposed configuration as the strongest member of the family on these benchmarks; consistent with the contributions stated in Section I, the value of this work lies in the combined region–boundary evaluation and the ranking disagreement it reveals, in the comparison of the proposed model with the standard baselines, and in the controlled analysis of the three components, rather than in a claim that the proposed configuration is the best available model. The qualitative observations of Section V-D rest on a small number of validation-split examples and are illustrative only.

# VII. Conclusion

The delineation of the lesion margin is the principal difficulty of dermoscopic segmentation, and it is the aspect of the task least well served by the region-overlap criteria with which segmentation methods are conventionally optimised and assessed. This work has investigated a targeted dual-stream refinement of the fusion stage of the MAFF-Net framework, directed at that aspect. The pretrained encoder, the convolutional decoder, and the auxiliary reconstruction branch are retained from the Original MAFF-Net, and the redesign is confined to the pathway in which spatial and spectral information are combined. In place of a single fusion route, the multi-scale features produced by the frozen backbone are processed along two parallel streams: a spatial stream that preserves regional and contextual structure, and a parameter-free Frequency Branch that provides an explicit spectral route for boundary-sensitive content. A Feature Alignment Module reconciles the statistics of the two representations, and a Gated Fusion Module combines them by a learned, spatially varying weighting. Training is conducted under a region objective supplemented by auxiliary boundary-focused supervision.

The method was evaluated on three public dermoscopic benchmarks against four segmentation architectures trained and assessed under an identical protocol, using region-based and boundary-based measures in combination. All members of the MAFF-Net family exceeded every baseline on both families of measures across all three datasets. A leave-one-out ablation of the three proposed components established that disabling any one of them degrades all three boundary measures simultaneously, while leaving the region measures substantially unaffected; the complete model did not record the highest value on any region measure. The improvements associated with these components, relative to the configurations in which they are disabled, are therefore confined to the fidelity of the predicted contour, and the results do not support, and this work does not advance, any claim that the components improve region overlap. It should further be recorded that the proposed configuration was not the strongest member of the MAFF-Net family on these benchmarks, the restored variant recording the higher value on the majority of the measures reported.

Stated conservatively, the contribution of this work is threefold. First, the evaluation of the models on three public dermoscopic benchmarks with region-based and boundary-based measures in combination shows that the two families of measures induce different rankings, so that region-overlap criteria alone are insufficient to characterise methods intended to act upon the lesion margin. Second, the proposed dual-stream model attains higher region-based and boundary-based scores than four standard convolutional and transformer baselines on all three benchmarks under an identical single-run protocol. Third, the dual-stream reformulation of the fusion pathway is investigated and the marginal role of each of its three components is isolated through a controlled leave-one-out ablation, whose results confine the effect of these components to boundary fidelity rather than to the extent of the predicted region. No comparison was drawn against results reported in the literature, and no claim of state-of-the-art performance is made.

The principal limitations of the study indicate the directions in which it should be extended. Every configuration was trained once, with a single experimental seed, and no statistical analysis was undertaken; repetition across several seeds, with appropriate interval estimates, is required before the smaller differences reported here can be regarded as established, and this is the most immediate priority. The evaluation is confined to three benchmarks drawn from a single imaging modality and annotation protocol, so assessment on further dermoscopic collections, and on other modalities and acquisition conditions, is needed to determine the generality of the findings. Finally, the boundary-focused supervision terms were active in every configuration and were not ablated, so their individual contribution remains unmeasured; ablating these terms, and examining alternative boundary-supervision formulations and their interaction with the architectural components, is a natural continuation of the present study.

## 3.4 Boundary-aware Deep Supervision

This subsection introduces the first part of our contribution, Boundary-aware Deep Supervision (BDS), which extends the restored MAFF-Net. BDS acts at the level of the training objective and does not modify the network: it adds no module, no parameter, and no inference-time computation, so the model evaluated at test time remains the restored base described in the preceding subsections.

Region-overlap objectives such as Dice loss and binary cross-entropy assign equal weight to every pixel. Because the lesion boundary occupies only a thin band of the image while the interior occupies the bulk, the gradient produced by these objectives is dominated by interior pixels. A model trained under overlap supervision alone can therefore reach a low loss while leaving the margin smooth and approximate, which is exactly the region where dermoscopic diagnosis is most sensitive. BDS adds a signal that measures error where it matters, by comparing the boundary of the prediction with the boundary of the ground truth directly.

Boundary information is obtained with a fixed edge operator rather than a learned one, so that no parameters are introduced. Let $y$ be the ground-truth mask and $\sigma(p)$ the predicted probability map for a given output. The Sobel operator $S(\cdot)$ is defined as the gradient magnitude computed from the horizontal and vertical Sobel kernels and clamped to $[0,1]$; applied to a binary or near-binary map it produces a thin response along the contour. The ground-truth boundary is then $S(y)$ and the predicted boundary is $S(\sigma(p))$. To keep the predicted boundary stable when the probability map is sharp, the prediction is lightly smoothed by local average pooling before the operator is applied. The contour can optionally be widened into a narrow band by dilation to allow a small spatial tolerance, with the band width treated as a hyperparameter.

The restored decoder already produces a segmentation prediction at each of its scales for deep supervision, and BDS reuses these outputs rather than adding new ones. For the $i$-th prediction $p_i$, the boundary term is the mean absolute difference between the predicted and ground-truth boundaries,

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BDS}}^{(i)} = \big\lVert\, S(\tilde{\sigma}(p_i)) - S(y) \,\big\rVert_{1},$$

where $\tilde{\sigma}(p_i)$ denotes the smoothed prediction and every scale is compared against the boundary of the full-resolution mask. The per-scale terms are aggregated with the same deep-supervision weights $w_i$ used by the segmentation loss,

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BDS}} = \frac{1}{\sum_i w_i} \sum_i w_i\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{BDS}}^{(i)}.$$

The boundary term is introduced gradually. Applying edge supervision from the first iteration can be unstable while the predictions are still poorly formed, so its weight follows a short warmup,

$$\lambda_b(t) = \lambda_b^{\max}\,\min\!\Big(1,\ \tfrac{t}{T_{\text{warm}}}\Big),$$

with $t$ the current training epoch and $T_{\text{warm}}$ the warmup length. The term is added to the restored model's segmentation objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}$ as

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}} + \lambda_b(t)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{BDS}},$$

and the reconstruction term described in the next subsection is added alongside it.

By penalizing disagreement at the contour at every scale, BDS is intended to encourage predictions that follow the true margin geometry rather than a smoothed approximation of it, with the largest effect expected on lesions whose borders are diffuse or irregular and are therefore hardest to recover from region supervision alone. Because the term reuses outputs the decoder already produces and a parameter-free operator, this is achieved without any change to the network at inference. The size of the effect is assessed in the ablation study rather than asserted here.

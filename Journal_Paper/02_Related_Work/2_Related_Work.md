# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 CNN-Based Segmentation Methods

The encoder–decoder architecture introduced by Ronneberger et al. [1] reshaped biomedical image segmentation by establishing that spatial detail lost during encoding can be recovered through direct skip connections between matching encoder and decoder levels. What made U-Net practically useful was not only this structural choice but its training efficiency on small annotated datasets—a property directly relevant to medical imaging, where pixel-level annotations are expensive to acquire. Trained from scratch on a few hundred dermoscopic examples, the architecture produced competitive segmentation results that earlier fully convolutional networks required far larger corpora to approach.

The semantic gap that U-Net's direct skip connections introduce—pairing shallow, high-resolution encoder features with deep, semantically rich decoder features without intermediate reconciliation—became a recognized limitation once performance began to plateau on challenging lesion morphologies. UNet++ [13] addressed this by replacing direct skip connections with nested dense paths that interpolate feature representations progressively along the encoder–decoder depth axis, allowing encoder and decoder features to be reconciled at intermediate semantic levels rather than bridged in a single step. This reduced the fusion mismatch and improved sensitivity on complex boundaries. Recent boundary-guided variants such as BGFF-UNet [7] and efficient hybrid designs such as FastTrans-Net [8] extend this lineage further, using explicit boundary cues and multi-scale fusion respectively to improve segmentation accuracy on dermoscopic benchmarks.

The gains from these architectural refinements are real but bounded by a mechanism that none of them modify: max-pooling. Each pooling operation retains the spatially dominant activation and discards variation around it, acting as a local low-pass filter. High-frequency spatial content—the edge gradients and intensity transitions concentrated at lesion boundaries—is attenuated at every pooling stage. By the bottleneck, the spectral content that encodes structural margins has been substantially reduced relative to low-frequency region content. Decoder upsampling and skip connections reconstruct spatial resolution but not spectral content; the fine-scale frequency information that encodes boundary sharpness is not recoverable once pooling has removed it. Architectural innovations that operate on post-pooling feature maps, however carefully designed, cannot recover information that the encoding path has already discarded.

## 2.2 Attention-Based Segmentation Methods

Soft attention gates, introduced to segmentation by Oktay et al. [3], made skip connections selective. Rather than passing all encoder features uniformly to the decoder, attention gates conditioned on the decoder's feature map at each resolution learn a spatial weighting that suppresses background activations and amplifies lesion-relevant regions before fusion. On organ segmentation tasks, this reduced false-positive predictions in regions adjacent to the target structure and improved the signal-to-noise ratio of the information flowing through skip connections.

Transferred to skin lesion segmentation, the mechanism provides consistent benefit on well-defined lesion margins, where spatially informative encoder features exist to be selectively amplified. The limitation becomes apparent on diffuse margins and irregular borders, and it traces to what attention gates are designed to do. A spatial attention mechanism selects from the feature representations available to it at each scale—it is a selection operator, not a reconstruction operator. The feature maps it selects from have already undergone the pooling-induced frequency attenuation described above. Attention gates cannot amplify high-frequency edge information that pooling has removed; they can only suppress the noise remaining alongside an already-attenuated edge signal. Improved selectivity helps, but it does not close the boundary-precision gap that pooling creates.

## 2.3 Transformer and Foundation Model Approaches

Transformer architectures entered medical image segmentation with the claim that convolutional operations, despite large stacked receptive fields, approximate long-range spatial dependencies only locally in a way that self-attention does not. TransUNet [2] operationalized this by tokenizing intermediate feature maps from a convolutional backbone and processing them through transformer layers before decoding, combining the multi-scale local features of convolutional encoding with the global spatial relationships modeled by self-attention. On lesions where global positional context matters—large, anatomically symmetric structures—this produced more consistent predictions than U-Net or Attention U-Net, establishing the hybrid convolutional–transformer design as a practical template for medical segmentation.

The quadratic scaling of self-attention with token count bounds what this template can achieve at high spatial resolutions. Token sequences derived from dermoscopic images at diagnostic resolution are large enough that full-resolution self-attention exceeds standard GPU memory budgets, so TransUNet applies attention to a downsampled feature map. This bounds the token count but also bounds the spatial frequency of the relationships that self-attention can model—fine-scale boundary detail, most informative at the original resolution, is underrepresented in the attention's operating space. The tension is structural: the resolution that makes dermoscopic segmentation clinically meaningful is exactly the resolution at which standard self-attention becomes computationally prohibitive.

Foundation-model encoders address the data-efficiency problem that motivates domain-specific architectures. SAM [14], trained on over one billion mask annotations spanning a wide range of visual domains, provides a Vision-Transformer backbone whose intermediate representations generalize well across imaging modalities. A frozen SAM encoder supplies strong spatial priors to a task-specific decoder, substantially reducing the annotated data required for satisfactory performance, and its pretrained features carry boundary-sensitive representations that improve over randomly initialized encoders. The activation statistics of these features, however, reflect the natural-image distribution on which SAM was trained, and full fine-tuning of so large an encoder is computationally costly and prone to overfitting on limited medical data. This motivates lightweight adaptation that keeps the encoder frozen while bridging the natural-to-medical domain gap through small trainable modules, an approach adopted by recent SAM-based medical segmentation methods rather than wholesale fine-tuning.

## 2.4 Frequency-Domain Segmentation Methods

The connection between spectral representations and boundary preservation has been recognized in computer vision for decades, but its application to learned segmentation models is more recent. SpectFormer [5] demonstrated, for image-classification transformers, that replacing some attention layers with spectral mixing improves both accuracy and computational efficiency, processing global frequency information at a cost that scales linearithmically rather than quadratically with sequence length. Frequency-based masked image modeling [9] applied spectral representations as a self-supervised pretraining signal for medical segmentation, finding that models initialized with frequency-aware objectives recover boundary detail more reliably than spatially-masked pretraining alone. These results collectively indicate that frequency-domain information complements spatial attention rather than substituting for it.

Applied directly to skin lesion segmentation, SGT-Net [6] developed a spectral generalized transformer that integrates FFT-based feature extraction into the segmentation pipeline. By explicitly processing boundary-frequency coefficients rather than allowing pooling to attenuate them, SGT-Net reported sensitivity gains over spatial architectures on ISIC benchmarks, confirming that spectral representations capture lesion edge structure that attention-based spatial encoders systematically miss. Frequency-aware feature fusion frameworks [15] reached similar conclusions in broader medical segmentation settings, finding that high-frequency spectral features carry complementary information that improves predictions most on structures with fine or irregular boundaries.

MAFF-Net [10] developed frequency fusion into a complete segmentation framework built on a frozen SAM image encoder. It adapts the frozen encoder with a Mixed Multi-scale Perception Adaptor (MMPA) that provides lightweight, scale-adaptive feature adaptation; complements the spatial stream with a Frequency-Guided Feature Fusion (FGFF) module that retains FFT-derived high-frequency content rather than discarding it; and adds an auxiliary Reconstruction Decoder Block (RDB) that imposes a self-supervised reconstruction constraint on the encoder features. The authors reported improvements in Dice and IoU over prior spatial baselines on ISIC benchmarks, supporting the expected benefit of joint spatial–spectral modeling.

Two considerations qualify the use of MAFF-Net as a basis for further study. First, a public reference implementation suitable for controlled extension is not readily available, and reproducing the framework requires attention to numerical detail; in particular, computing the FFT on half-precision tensors under automatic mixed-precision training is unstable and is reliably avoided only by isolating the spectral computation to single precision. Second, and more substantively for the present work, MAFF-Net's supervision remains region- and reconstruction-oriented: the segmentation objective rewards interior overlap and the reconstruction objective rewards global appearance, while the lesion margin—the property most consequential for diagnosis—is supervised only indirectly.

## 2.5 Research Gap and Motivation

The paradigms reviewed above address complementary aspects of dermoscopic segmentation, each advancing the field while leaving specific problems unresolved. Convolutional encoder–decoders established efficient, stable pipelines but could not recover boundary detail that pooling had discarded. Attention mechanisms improved the fidelity of spatial feature transfer through skip connections, yet operated on post-pooling representations from which high-frequency edge content was already absent. Transformer-based models captured global spatial relationships but at a computational cost incompatible with the resolutions where boundary detail is encoded, and foundation-model encoders added strong pretrained priors that benefit most from lightweight, frozen-encoder adaptation rather than full fine-tuning. Frequency-guided architectures, including MAFF-Net [10], directly targeted the spectral information that spatial models suppress and reported boundary-precision gains, and we adopt this frequency-guided design as the base of our study.

A gap nonetheless persists at the level of supervision rather than architecture. Standard overlap-based objectives—Dice loss and binary cross-entropy—weight prediction error uniformly across spatial positions. Because lesion boundaries occupy only a small fraction of image pixels, these objectives are dominated by interior region predictions; a model converging under overlap supervision alone may yield smooth boundary predictions that score well on Dice while systematically misrepresenting the irregular margin geometry that distinguishes melanoma subtypes. The auxiliary reconstruction constraint employed by MAFF-Net regularizes feature learning toward global appearance, but it likewise does not direct the network specifically toward boundary fidelity. Across the frequency-guided methods reviewed here, explicit and direct penalization of margin prediction error—applied across the decoder's multi-scale outputs and propagated to the auxiliary reconstruction branch—is absent.

This observation defines the scope of the present work and its relationship to MAFF-Net. We first restore the original MAFF-Net components (frozen SAM2 encoder, Mixed Multi-scale Perception Adaptor, Frequency-Guided Feature Fusion, and Reconstruction Decoder Block) into a faithful, reproducible base with numerically stable spectral computation under mixed precision; this base reproduces the published design and is not claimed as a contribution. We then address the supervision gap through Boundary-centric Dual Supervision, which derives a single boundary prior from the ground-truth mask and uses it to supervise margins from two complementary directions—Boundary-aware Deep Supervision at the decoder's multi-scale prediction heads, and Edge-weighted Reconstruction in the auxiliary reconstruction branch. The proposal acts at the level of the training objective alone, leaving the restored architecture and its inference cost unchanged, and targets a documented limitation rather than adding architectural complexity for its own sake.

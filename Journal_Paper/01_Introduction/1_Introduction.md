# 1. Introduction

Melanoma accounts for a disproportionately large share of skin-cancer mortality relative to its incidence, and its diagnosis depends heavily on the stage at which a lesion is detected: localized disease carries a far higher five-year survival rate than distant-stage disease [20]. Dermoscopy, the magnified polarized-light imaging of the skin surface, improves clinician accuracy over naked-eye examination, but it also shifts diagnostic weight onto precise lesion characterization that scales poorly in mass screening. Computer-aided detection systems built around accurate lesion segmentation offer a practical route to closing that gap, linking pixel-level boundary delineation to downstream feature extraction, severity staging, and treatment planning. Boundary imprecision is not merely an algorithmic inconvenience, because even modest margin errors can reclassify lesion severity and alter clinical management.

Segmenting skin lesions from dermoscopic images is harder than benchmark scores sometimes suggest. Lesions vary widely in shape, pigmentation, and texture; the transition from lesion to surrounding skin is frequently gradual rather than abrupt; and dermoscopic artifacts such as terminal hair, specular reflections, and peripheral gel introduce local intensity patterns that overlap spatially with genuine boundary signals. Models that optimize primarily for pixel-level overlap can attain competitive Dice scores while producing spatially smooth predictions that misrepresent margin geometry, eroding the irregular border detail that distinguishes early melanoma from benign dysplastic nevi. Boundary fidelity, rather than interior fill accuracy, is therefore the operative criterion for diagnostic utility.

Deep learning for biomedical segmentation has been dominated by the encoder–decoder framework of U-Net [1], whose skip connections preserve spatial detail and which trains efficiently on small annotated datasets. Subsequent work refined this design: Attention U-Net [3] introduced soft attention gates that suppress irrelevant activations at skip connections, and UNet++ [13] added nested dense paths to reduce the semantic gap between encoder and decoder features. These extensions improve within-distribution accuracy but do not remove the mechanism that limits boundary fidelity. Successive max-pooling acts as a local low-pass filter, progressively attenuating the high-frequency components that encode edge gradients; by the bottleneck, this structural margin information has been diminished relative to low-frequency region content, and decoder upsampling cannot recover spectral content that the encoder has already suppressed. Predictions are consequently accurate on lesion interiors yet unreliable at margins.

Transformer-based models offer a complementary path. TransUNet [2] tokenizes intermediate convolutional features and applies multi-head self-attention to model long-range dependencies, recovering global consistency that convolutional receptive fields cannot capture in a single pass. The difficulty is that self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length, so at dermoscopic resolutions the token sequences are too long for full-attention computation on standard hardware; TransUNet therefore applies attention to a downsampled feature map, which limits gains on the fine-scale boundary structure that matters most clinically.

Foundation-model encoders provide a third path. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) [14], pretrained on more than one billion masks, yields a Vision-Transformer encoder whose representations generalize across visual domains, and its successor SAM2 [16] extends this capability with a hierarchical (Hiera) backbone. A frozen SAM-family encoder supplies strong spatial priors and substantially reduces the labeled data required for task adaptation. However, such encoders are designed for prompt-driven interactive segmentation rather than fully automated, boundary-sensitive medical analysis, and full fine-tuning is computationally costly and prone to overfitting on limited medical data. This motivates lightweight adaptation that keeps the encoder frozen while bridging the natural-to-medical domain gap through small trainable modules.

The pooling-induced loss of boundary frequencies motivates frequency-guided architectures. The two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform decomposes feature maps into spectral coefficients ordered by frequency, allowing the high-frequency content that encodes edges to be retained rather than discarded. SGT-Net [6] applied a spectral generalized transformer to this end, and MAFF-Net [10] integrated frequency processing into a complete framework built on a frozen SAM image encoder: it adapts the encoder with a Mixed Multi-scale Perception Adaptor (MMPA), complements spatial features with a Frequency-Guided Feature Fusion (FGFF) module, and adds an auxiliary Reconstruction Decoder Block (RDB) for self-supervised regularization. Within this design, however, supervision rewards interior overlap and global reconstruction fidelity, while the lesion margin is supervised only indirectly.

This study takes MAFF-Net as its base and proceeds in two clearly separated stages. First, because a public reference implementation suitable for controlled extension is not readily available, we re-implement the original MAFF-Net components faithfully: a frozen SAM2 (Hiera) image encoder [16], the dynamic Mixed Multi-scale Perception Adaptor, the cross-modal Frequency-Guided Feature Fusion module, and the Reconstruction Decoder Block. As part of this restoration, the fast Fourier transform is isolated to single precision within an otherwise mixed-precision pipeline, since half-precision spectral computation is prone to numerical overflow. This restored model reproduces the original architecture and serves only as the experimental base; it is not claimed as a contribution. Second, on this base we introduce a supervision-level extension, Boundary-Centric Dual Supervision, which derives a single boundary prior from the ground-truth mask and applies it in two complementary ways: Boundary-aware Deep Supervision enforces edge agreement at the decoder's multi-scale prediction heads, and Edge-weighted Reconstruction steers the auxiliary reconstruction toward lesion-boundary regions. Both terms modify only the training objective and leave the inference-time model identical to the restored base.

We evaluate the restored base and the proposed supervision on three ISIC dermoscopic benchmarks that differ in size, diagnostic category distribution, and annotation protocol—ISIC 2016 [11], ISIC 2017 [12], and ISIC 2018 [4]—against U-Net [1], Attention U-Net [3], UNet++ [13], and TransUNet [2], using Dice, IoU, sensitivity, and specificity, with the effect of each proposed component assessed through a controlled ablation under a uniform training protocol.

To keep the relationship to MAFF-Net unambiguous, we separate the original design, our reproduction of it, and our proposed extension. The contributions of this paper are as follows.

1. *(Restored base, not claimed as a contribution.)* A faithful, reproducible re-implementation of the original MAFF-Net—frozen SAM2 image encoder, dynamic Mixed Multi-scale Perception Adaptor, cross-modal Frequency-Guided Feature Fusion, and Reconstruction Decoder Block—with numerically stable spectral computation under mixed-precision training. This restored model is presented as the experimental base.

2. *(Proposed contribution.)* Boundary-aware Deep Supervision, an explicit multi-scale edge-agreement objective applied at the deep-supervision heads of the restored decoder, which directly penalizes margin imprecision.

3. *(Proposed contribution.)* Edge-weighted Reconstruction, a boundary-reweighted formulation of the auxiliary reconstruction objective that couples the reconstruction branch to lesion margins.

4. *(Proposed contribution.)* A unified Boundary-Centric Dual Supervision objective in which both terms are driven by a single ground-truth boundary prior, together with a controlled ablation of the restored base and each proposed component on three ISIC benchmarks.
